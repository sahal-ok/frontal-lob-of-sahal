A thought experiment about the future

The Next
Revolution

What if every major economic revolution is really the same story? We optimize one capability, make it cheaper and more accessible, and then discover a new bottleneck.

Scroll to explore
00 — THE QUESTION

What connects the revolutions?

Predicting the future is extremely difficult.

If someone could consistently predict what would happen next, they would probably become one of the richest people in history.

So instead of asking exactly what the future will look like, let's ask a different question:

What is humanity continuously trying to optimize?

And more importantly:

What becomes the next bottleneck after that optimization succeeds?
01 — THE PATTERN

Agriculture → Industry → Internet → AI → Agents

Maybe these aren't completely separate revolutions.

Maybe they are different stages of the same process: making previously expensive capabilities cheap and accessible.

01

Agriculture

Optimize food production.

02

Industry

Optimize physical power.

03

Internet

Optimize access to knowledge.

04

AI

Optimize cognitive capability.

05

Agents

Optimize autonomous execution.

02 — AGRICULTURE

Cheap food changed the world.

Humans learned how to systematically produce food instead of depending entirely on hunting and gathering.

Better crops, seeds, irrigation, fertilizers, domestication and agricultural techniques dramatically increased the amount of food that could be produced.

Optimization

Agriculture optimized the production of food per unit of human effort.

But optimization exposed another constraint: human physical labor.

03 — INDUSTRY

Cheap muscle.

The Industrial Revolution attacked the physical bottleneck.

Steam engines, factories, electricity, vehicles and machines allowed humans to multiply their physical capability.

Instead of asking how many humans we need, we could ask how powerful a machine we could put behind the task.

Physical production became dramatically cheaper and more scalable.

The new bottleneck

We could produce things. But increasingly complex systems required something else: knowledge.

04 — INTERNET

Cheap access to knowledge.

The Internet dramatically reduced the cost of accessing information.

A person sitting in a small town could suddenly access books, research, lectures, documentation, experts and communities from around the world.

Knowledge became dramatically more accessible.

The Internet didn't just connect computers. It democratized access to knowledge.

But access to information is not the same as the ability to use it.

The bottleneck moved again.

From access to knowledge → to the ability to understand and apply it.
05 — AI

Cheap intelligence.

AI may represent another reduction in the cost of capability.

The Internet gave people access to enormous amounts of information. AI increasingly gives people systems that can reason over that information, generate ideas, write code, analyze data and perform cognitive tasks.

Intelligence itself is becoming cheaper to access.

But once intelligence becomes cheap, another question appears:

What happens when thinking becomes cheaper than executing?

06 — AGENTS

From intelligence to action.

Thinking isn't the same as doing.

An AI can tell you how to find customers. An agent can attempt to actually find them.

01

Research

Find potential customers.

02

Generate

Create personalized messages.

03

Execute

Send messages and perform actions.

04

Measure

Track responses and results.

05

Improve

Change the strategy based on the outcome.

Now imagine 100 agents.

Or 10,000.

At that scale, the problem isn't simply intelligence anymore.

The problem becomes coordination.
07 — THE NEXT BOTTLENECK

Optimization of optimization.

Imagine a company with 100,000 AI agents.

Some research. Some code. Some sell. Some negotiate. Some monitor machines. Some analyze customers.

You cannot manually coordinate all of them.

You need a system that decides:

01
Which agent should perform which task?
02
When should it act?
03
Which agents should work together?
04
When should a human intervene?
05
How do we measure whether an agent actually created value?
08 — RESPONSIBILITY

The next scarcity may be responsibility.

Imagine a world where creating things becomes extremely cheap.

Software becomes cheap. Research becomes cheap. Marketing becomes cheap. Design becomes cheap. Agents become cheap.

Then simply doing something may no longer be the scarce capability.

Who is responsible for making sure the thing actually works?

An AI might be able to build a website. Another might create advertisements. Another might contact customers.

But who is responsible when the business fails?

The question changes from:

"Can you perform the task?"

to:

"Can you reliably produce the desired outcome?"
09 — OUTCOMES

People don't buy technology. They buy outcomes.

Imagine two people.

Person A says:

"I built an AI agent."

Person B says:

"I increased this company's sales using AI agents."

These are very different economic propositions.

The first sells capability.

The second sells an outcome.

Technology is the mechanism.

The outcome is what matters.

As capabilities become cheaper, reliably producing outcomes may become more valuable.

10 — HUMANS

What happens to human work?

When automobiles became widespread, the number of horses used for transportation declined dramatically.

We didn't simply replace horses with another kind of horse.

The entire transportation system changed.

The same question can be asked about robots:

If robots can perform physical work, why would humans need to perform that work?

Some jobs may disappear. Some may shrink. Others may change completely.

But technological revolutions also create new environments, and new environments create new problems.

11 — THE NEW FRONTIER

The frontier keeps moving.

If robots eventually perform most ordinary physical work, humanity may move toward problems that are currently inaccessible.

Space exploration. Scientific discovery. New engineering. New technologies. New environments. Problems that don't exist yet.

These are examples, not predictions.

When one frontier becomes cheap, humans tend to move toward another.
12 — WEALTH CREATION

Why do people pay you?

Perhaps the simplest answer is:

People have wants, but there is a gap between what they want and their ability to obtain it.

That gap is where businesses exist.

Someone wants software but doesn't know how to build it. You build it.

Someone wants an AI agent but doesn't know how to create or deploy it. You build it.

Someone wants something listed on Google Maps but doesn't know how to get it there. You solve the problem.

Find the gap.

Find something people want but don't know how to obtain, and build the bridge between the two.

13 — DEMOCRATIZATION

Optimization isn't the whole story.

There is another recurring pattern:

Democratization.

Agriculture democratized the ability to produce food.

Industry democratized access to mechanical power.

The Internet democratized access to information.

AI is democratizing access to cognitive capability.

Agents may democratize autonomous execution.

Whatever comes next may democratize something we currently consider expensive, specialized or difficult.

14 — THE FRAMEWORK

How to think about the future.

01
What is currently expensive?
02
What would happen if it became 100× cheaper?
03
Who would gain access?
04
What new things would people do with that capability?
05
What bottleneck would appear after that?

Don't ask what the future will be.

Ask:
What is becoming cheap?

And:
What will become scarce because of it?

15 — THE 1% GUESS

A tiny bet on the future.

This framework cannot tell us exactly what the future will look like.

The future is too complex for that.

But if I had to make a tiny bet—not a prediction, just a 1% probability guess—I would look for technologies that do one of two things:

01

Optimize an existing capability.

02

Democratize a capability that is currently expensive or difficult to access.

And then I would look one step further.

Every successful optimization creates a new bottleneck.

Maybe the future belongs less to the people who can predict exactly what happens next...

...and more to the people who can recognize the next bottleneck when it appears.

The future may not be about predicting the next revolution.

It may be about finding the next thing that needs optimization.

A thought experiment about optimization, democratization and the next bottleneck.
